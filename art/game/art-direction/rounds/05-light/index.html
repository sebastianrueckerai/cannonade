## Director's read, provisional

The relights kept every object and the camera in all five. What moved is the light, the sky, the sea and, in two frames, the palette.

- **1 noon**: the anchor's light. Short hard shadows, a plain sky, a flat sea. Everything reads. The base shadow under a wall is one tile short, so the cream wall against the sand path is at its weakest here.
- **2 morning**: a low sun from the left, long soft shadows to the right, cool pale light, a glare in the top-left corner and a sparkle on the sea. The long shadows separate every wall from its ground, and they also fall across tiles the player must read. The saturation drops a step.
- **3 afternoon**: a sun from the front right, medium shadows, warm light on the lit faces, a cream fog band at the horizon, the sea flat blue. The walls separate from the sand by warm against cool, the grid stays, nothing of the board is lost. The brief's saturated and cosy light.
- **4 fogged**: depth fog from mid-board, the far pier and the ships half gone, the island floating in cream. The mood of A Monster's Expedition, and on a 30 tile board the far rows the player builds on would be in the fog at the default view.
- **5 overcast**: no sun, no shadows. The sea turned grey-blue, a drift from the palette. The walls still read by their lit top against their faces, and the sand edge is the worst of the six: nothing separates the wall base from the path. The control case: the shadows carry the readability.
- **6 evening**: an orange sky and a sea full of streaks. The water became a painting that competes with the island, the grass went olive and the stone pink, the cannons inside the yard are the hardest to count. A store still, not the game.

Director's ranking for the game's default view: 3, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6. The afternoon light with the horizon fog band.

## Faults in the images

- 5 and 6 moved the palette: the sea in 5, the grass and the stone in 6. A relight must not recolour the surfaces, which argues for the game doing the lighting in the engine from the rules, never from a frame.
- 2 put a sun glare into the frame corner, an object the prompt did not ask for.
- 4's fog starts too near. The rule puts the fog start past the far edge of the buildable board.

Critiques

gemini (google/gemini-2.5-pro)
1.  **Deviations and Readability Issues:**
    *   **2 (morning):** The "light haze" described is a thick, opaque fog band at the horizon. At 13px, the soft shadows blur the bases of walls, and the lower contrast makes the cannons less distinct.
    *   **3 (afternoon):** The "cream fog" is a hard-edged band, not a soft gradient. At 13px, the warm light slightly merges the orange roofs with the sunlit cream walls.
    *   **4 (fogged):** The fog starts much closer than the "far edge," washing out the front beach tiles. At 13px, the fog completely removes the base shadows from the far walls, making them illegible.
    *   **5 (overcast):** The palette has shifted to be cool and desaturated, losing the "cheerful, sunlit" feel. At 13px, the lack of cast shadows makes it impossible to distinguish walls from the ground, rendering the forms flat and unreadable.
    *   **6 (evening):** The "orange sky" has resulted in an extreme orange colour grade across the entire scene, not just the sky. At 13px, the dark, saturated shadows completely obscure the cannons and the team-coloured bases.

2.  **Specific Readability Tests at 13 Pixels:**
    *   **Wall from sand:** Lightings **1 and 3** maintain a clear separation with a crisp shadow. Lightings **2, 4, 5, and 6** lose it due to soft shadows, fog, no shadows, or colour blending, respectively.
    *   **Tile grid on grass:** Lightings **1, 3, and 5** keep the grid visible. Lightings **2, 4, and 6** lose it to low contrast, fog, or overwhelming coloured light and shadow.
    *   **Cannons:** Lighting **6** makes the cannons hardest to count, as they become black silhouettes lost in near-black shadows.

3.  **The Sea:**
    *   The water reads as a flat, tiled part of the board in frames **1 and 5**.
    *   It becomes a competing painterly element in frames **3** (due to the stylized fog band and shoreline gradient) and **6** (due to the dramatic, high-contrast ripples and reflections).

4.  **The Fog:**
    *   Fog helps when it starts past the island, creating atmospheric depth without impeding gameplay, as suggested by the horizon haze in frame **2**.
    *   Fog eats the playable area in frame **4**, where it begins close to the camera and washes out the far side of the island, making tiles there unreadable.

5.  **Lighting Rules:**
    1.  **Sun:** Elevation between 45-70 degrees; azimuth between 45-135 degrees (relative to the camera's forward direction) to create clear side-lighting on forms.
    2.  **Shadows:** Strength between 0.6-0.8 to be dark enough for definition but not so black that they hide details within them.
    3.  **Fog:** Start distance at >0.7 of the board depth, ending at 1.0, to ensure the entire playable island is always clear.

6.  **Ranking for Default View:**
    1.  The best for pure readability, with every element clearly defined by hard, neutral shadows.
    2.  Excellent readability with more pleasing atmosphere, though the warm light is a minor risk for colour confusion.
    3.  Good, but the soft shadows begin to compromise the sharp, chunky definition required at small sizes.
    4.  Poor readability, as the fog obscures the back of the playable area and flattens crucial details.
    5.  Extremely poor readability, as the overwhelming colour and deep shadows make interior details invisible.
    6.  Unplayable, as the lack of cast shadows makes it impossible to read 3D forms, heights, and placement.

    RANKING: 1, 3, 2, 4, 6, 5

**1 noon:** Perfect readability—hard shadows anchor every wall, cannons are distinct individuals, the tile grid is razor‑sharp, and the palette stays pure and cheerful; the uncontested functional winner.

**5 overcast:** Unusable at the target resolution—zero shadows cause walls to merge with terrain, cannons to become an uncountable blob, and the tile grid to disappear entirely; it fails the core readability requirement despite being inoffensive in style.
