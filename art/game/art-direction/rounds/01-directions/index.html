# Round 01 notes

Models per frame from the probe: nano-banana for world and cannon, ideogram for menu. 15 images, 1.95 USD. Critics in [critique/](critique): Gemini 2.5 Pro and GLM, the latter after Sebastian topped up Z.AI the same evening. GPT-5 is not reachable through fal any-llm any more.

## Director's read, provisional

1. **signal-flags**: the most readable world frame and the most distinct title. The cannon is too multicoloured for one unit, in the game the team colour takes that role. The menu buttons are gibberish and the layout is weak.
2. **ink-chart**: the most original identity and the best fit for a fortress-on-a-map game and for a browser demo. Needs an outline shader in Godot, which is one extra piece of tech. The paper sea needs a rule for ships.
3. **toy-harbour**: the most finished-looking set and the best menu. Also the closest to the low-poly island look every asset pack has. The red roofs fight the red team highlight.
4. **weathered-stone**: the world reads well but says nothing. The menu is photoreal and does not belong.
5. **lantern-dusk**: beautiful stills, unreadable at 13 px, team colours drown.

Gemini ranked signal-flags, ink-chart, toy-harbour, weathered-stone, lantern-dusk. Same order as the director. GLM ranked signal-flags, toy-harbour, ink-chart, weathered-stone, lantern-dusk: same first place, same last two, second and third swapped. First calibration point for roadmap A6. Sebastian's pick is the one that counts.

Critiques

gemini (google/gemini-2.5-pro)
Of course. As an independent art critic, I will provide a thorough analysis of the five proposed art directions for your low-poly browser strategy game.

Here is my assessment of each direction based on the provided brief.

---

### Direction 1: ink-chart

*   **Identity:** This direction establishes its uniqueness by emulating a hand-drawn technical illustration or a cartographer's map, using clean ink outlines and flat watercolour-style fills.
*   **Readability at distance:** Excellent. The strong, dark outlines will ensure that even a 13-pixel-tall wall retains a crisp, defined silhouette against the ground and other objects, making structures and ownership instantly legible.
*   **Consistency:** The style is exceptionally consistent across the detailed cannon, the UI map, and the in-game world, all feeling like components of a single, beautifully drafted document.
*   **Risk:** The biggest risk is the technical implementation and artistic cost of the outlines; getting them to look consistently clean on 60 different assets, without visual artifacting or becoming too thick on smaller objects, is a significant production challenge.
*   **Recommended Edit:** Make the screen-space outlines a fixed pixel width. This ensures they provide clear definition on distant objects without becoming overwhelmingly thick on objects close to the camera, maintaining perfect readability at all zoom levels.

### Direction 2: lantern-dusk

*   **Identity:** This direction sets itself apart with a moody, high-contrast nocturnal atmosphere, using dramatic lighting to carve shapes out of the darkness.
*   **Readability at distance:** Very poor. The dark-on-dark palette and reliance on complex lighting means that walls and cannons will dissolve into murky shapes. Distinguishing between deep blue, purple, and olive team colours in this environment would be nearly impossible, violating a core requirement of the brief.
*   **Consistency:** The three frames are perfectly consistent, all sharing the same dramatic lighting, deep shadows, and cool colour palette, evoking a cohesive sense of nighttime siege.
*   **Risk:** The style is entirely dependent on bespoke, artistic lighting that fights directly against gameplay clarity. Every level would require intensive lighting work, and the system would be too inflexible to clearly display team ownership or other essential game state information.
*   **One recommended edit:** Shift the setting from night to a "magic hour" (dawn or dusk). This would retain the long, dramatic shadows and atmosphere but introduce enough warm ambient light to make neutral fortress colours distinct and team colours readable.

### Direction 3: signal-flags

*   **Identity:** This direction presents a striking, minimalist aesthetic that abstracts the concept of a fortress into a language of bold, simple geometric forms and primary colours, reminiscent of naval signal flags.
*   **Readability at distance:** Superb. The use of large, un-textured, colour-blocked shapes is ideal for legibility at small sizes. Walls will be clean rectangles, and team-coloured cannons will pop against the neutral environment, making the game state readable at a glance.
*   **Consistency:** The direction is rigorously consistent, with the hyper-clean models, flat shading, and sharp, modern UI typography all contributing to a single, unified design language.
*   **Risk:** The primary risk is that the style's minimalism could be perceived as overly sterile or cheap. Without careful attention to secondary details and animation, the world may lack the charm and character needed to engage players long-term.
*   **One recommended edit:** Add subtle, non-interfering secondary details and animations, such as stylised wind lines, gentle procedural waves, or bouncy "pop" animations for placing units, to inject life and personality without compromising the clean aesthetic.

### Direction 4: toy-harbour

*   **Identity:** This direction establishes a charming and friendly identity by rendering the world as a high-quality painted wooden toy set, with soft, rounded forms and a warm, vibrant palette.
*   **Readability at distance:** Good. While more detailed than `signal-flags`, the models are still chunky and use clear colour-blocking. The bright, even lighting ensures that team colours will read well, and the simple silhouettes of the walls and cannons will hold up at a small scale.
*   **Consistency:** The cannon, menu, and world view all feel like parts of the same playset. The menu itself looks like a "box art" version of the game, creating excellent cohesion between the game and its potential store presence.
*   **Risk:** The rounded, "soft" aesthetic might visually clash with the destructive, competitive nature of a Rampart-style game. The primary challenge will be making actions like cannon fire and wall destruction feel satisfying without breaking the cheerful, toy-like feel.
*   **One recommended edit:** Emphasise the "toy" physics in animations. When a wall is destroyed, have it break apart into clean, blocky pieces that bounce and settle like toy blocks, rather than crumbling like real stone, to make destruction feel impactful yet stylistically appropriate.

### Direction 5: weathered-stone

*   **Identity:** This direction carves out its niche with a more grounded, semi-realistic feel, focusing on the texture and form of worn, chunky stone to create a sense of history and weight.
*   **Readability at distance:** Poor. The monochromatic, low-contrast stone texture will cause walls to blend together into a grey mass. While individual cannons are clear, distinguishing a blue cannon from a purple one against this visually noisy, dark background would be difficult from the gameplay camera angle.
*   **Consistency:** There is a significant inconsistency between the UI and the game world. The menu uses a gritty, photorealistic image of a cliffside fortress that clashes with the cleaner, more stylised low-poly models of the game itself, creating a jarring disconnect.
*   **Risk:** This direction is the most likely to become "generic low poly." The focus on realism without fully committing to it results in a style that lacks a strong, memorable hook and risks looking dated or like a collection of un-styled store assets.
*   **One recommended edit:** Replace the flat, noisy textures with strong bevels and defined planes on the models. This would allow the geometry itself to catch light and create definition, improving readability and pushing the style away from genericism towards a more deliberate, chunky look.

---

### Final Ranking

1.  **signal-flags:** This is the strongest direction, as it is built from the ground up for maximum gameplay clarity, which is paramount in a strategy game.
2.  **ink-chart:** A close second, offering excellent readability with a uniquely stylish and memorable identity that strongly supports the "game on a map" feel.
3.  **toy-harbour:** A very strong contender that is highly readable and has a charming, marketable identity, though its tone may be slightly at odds with the gameplay.
4.  **weathered-stone:** This direction struggles with readability and has a critical consistency problem between its UI and world art that undermines its identity.
5.  **lantern-dusk:** While visually striking in a still frame, it fundamentally fails the brief's most important requirement for readability and clear team colours.

**RANKING:** signal-flags, ink-chart, toy-harbour, weathered-stone, lantern-dusk
glm (glm-5v-turbo)
**ink-chart**
- **Identity:** A cartographic “living map” aesthetic where every element—terrain, structures, UI—looks hand-drawn on aged parchment with compass roses and nautical ink lines, making the game feel like a strategic tabletop war game come to life.
- **Readability at distance:** Moderate risk; the strong grid lines and dark silhouettes help, but the paper texture and subtle sepia toning may cause low-contrast structures (especially olive or teal teams) to blur into the background when a wall is only 13 px tall.
- **Consistency:** High—the cannon appears as an ink illustration on the same stock as the world map, and the menu’s wax-seal/compass motif ties all three frames into a unified swashbuckling atlas.
- **Risk:** **Texture overload.** When sixty cannons, walls, and ships are placed, the hand-drawn noise and hatching will create visual static that obscures team ownership and grid boundaries, turning the board into an illegible mess.
- **Recommended edit:** Flatten the terrain to a solid cream/off-white (removing the parchment grain) while keeping the ink-line art style on units and structures; this preserves the cartographic identity without fighting the 13‑px resolution limit.

**lantern-dusk**
- **Identity:** A cinematic, moody night-fortress atmosphere with dramatic volumetric lighting, deep indigo skies, and warm amber glow from windows and braziers, evoking a single-player stealth or adventure game rather than a multiplayer strategy title.
- **Readability at distance:** **Poor.** At 13 px per wall, the crushed blacks will make deep blue and purple teams indistinguishable from shadows, and the beautiful ambient occlusion will turn all geometry into undifferentiated dark blobs.
- **Consistency:** Good between world and menu (shared lighting rig and castle model), but the cannon frame feels like a product render dropped into a different universe—it lacks the environmental storytelling of the other two shots.
- **Risk:** **Legibility suicide.** Strategy requires reading the board state in a glance; this direction prioritizes mood over information density, guaranteeing that players cannot quickly identify which cannon belongs to which team during fast-paced multiplayer.
- **Recommended edit:** Switch to a “twilight” key with much brighter ambient light (50% brighter) and force all team-owned objects to emit a colored glow (blue/purple/teal/olive) so ownership reads even in dim conditions.

**signal-flags**
- **Identity:** A bold, graphic, board-game-inspired direction using flat unshaded colors, crisp geometric facets, and high-contrast team coding that feels like a digital version of a high-end European strategy board game (think *Catan* meets *Clash Royale*’s clarity).
- **Readability at distance:** **Excellent.** The saturated, distinct color blocks (red bases, blue cannons, yellow earth) and sharp edges will remain crisply legible at 13 px, and the four required team colors will pop immediately against the neutral light-grey sky.
- **Consistency:** **Perfect.** The cannon’s faceted low-poly style matches the world’s isometric blocks exactly, and the menu uses the same typeface, button treatment, and color vocabulary without feeling like a separate “UI skin.”
- **Risk:** **Generic “asset store” fatigue.** While functional, the style is safe and could feel sterile or cheap if the 60 assets don’t receive enough unique silhouette love, ending up looking like a reskin of a thousand other mobile strategy games.
- **Recommended edit:** Introduce a subtle “paper” or “screen-print” texture overlay on the UI and a slight chromatic aberration/rich black outline on all 3D models to give the flat style a tactile, premium print quality that elevates it above the norm.

**toy-harbour**
- **Identity:** A cheerful, Mediterranean “toy-town” aesthetic with rounded plastic-looking geometry, terracotta roofs, turquoise water, and a warm inviting palette that feels like opening a high-end wooden playset.
- **Readability at distance:** **Good.** The high saturation and rounded forms are clear, though the dominant warm orange/terracotta of the buildings may slightly desaturate or clash with the olive team color; teal and purple will read well against the sandy ground.
- **Consistency:** **Excellent.** All three frames share identical material properties (soft diffuse shading, no roughness maps), color temperature, and a playful “miniature” scale that makes the menu feel like part of the diorama.
- **Risk:** **Strategic trivialization.** The extreme roundness and “cute” factor may undermine the tension of a competitive artillery game, making destruction feel like knocking over toys rather than siege warfare; additionally, the bright cyan water may cause eye strain over long sessions.
- **Recommended edit:** Desaturate the water and sky by 20% to create a cooler backdrop that lets the warm units pop, and add a subtle grid line to the ground plane to reinforce the tactical, grid-based nature of the gameplay beneath the toy-like surface.

**weathered-stone**
- **Identity:** A grim, “realistic” low-poly attempt at a historical siege simulator using muted greys, stone photo-textures, and atmospheric fog, aiming for a serious *Age of Empires* or *Stronghold* vibe on a browser budget.
- **Readability at distance:** **Poor.** The desaturated grey palette will swallow the olive and teal team colors entirely, and at 13 px the detailed stone normal-maps will dissolve into noisy grey mud where individual walls are unreadable.
- **Consistency:** Moderate—the world and cannon share a material language, but the menu feels like a default Unity template pasted on top, lacking the atmospheric fog and texture work of the gameplay views.
- **Risk:** **The uncanny valley of low-fidelity realism.** By trying to be photorealistic without the polygon count, it risks looking dated (PS1-era) rather than stylized, and it directly violates the “nothing photoreal, nothing gritty” brief while failing the team-color distinction requirement.
- **Recommended edit:** Abandon the photo-textures entirely; shift to a **stylized stone** flat-color palette (cool grey, warm grey, slate) and use **brightly colored banners/flags** (the four team colors) as the primary ownership indicator, painted onto every structure in large 50%+ surface-area patches.

---

**Rankings**

1. **signal-flags:** This is the only direction that satisfies every hard constraint—flat colors, 13‑px readability, four-team clarity, cross-frame consistency—while still possessing a clean, graphic identity that scales perfectly to a browser window.
2. **toy-harbour:** It sacrifices a little tactical seriousness for a delightful, cohesive toy aesthetic that will retain players through charm, and it remains highly readable despite the warmer palette.
3. **ink-chart:** The cartographic concept is the most artistically distinctive and thematically fitting for a naval artillery game, but it courts disaster with texture clutter at small sizes and requires strict discipline to avoid becoming messy.
4. **weathered-stone:** It ignores the “no gritty/photoreal” rule, fails the team-color test on grey stone, and will look like a blurry mess at the required zoom level, offering only a derivative “realism” that low-poly cannot support.
5. **lantern-dusk:** A beautiful screenshot for an adventure game, but a catastrophic choice for a competitive strategy title where players must identify team ownership in milliseconds; the darkness makes it functionally unplayable at the specified scale.

RANKING: signal-flags, toy-harbour, ink-chart, weathered-stone, lantern-dusk
