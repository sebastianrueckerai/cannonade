# Round 02 notes

Phase A, mood and theme. Four candidate worlds in [themes.md](themes.md), six frames each through `google/nano-banana-2.1/edit` with the round 01 anchor and two or three references attached, see [round.json](round.json). 24 images, 3.60 USD, plus two critiques at 0.03 USD each. One board per candidate: [board-toy-harbour.jpg](board-toy-harbour.jpg), [board-lighthouse-islands.jpg](board-lighthouse-islands.jpg), [board-river-town.jpg](board-river-town.jpg), [board-shipwright-yard.jpg](board-shipwright-yard.jpg). The reference collage per candidate is `refs-<slug>.jpg`.

## Director's read, provisional

1. **lighthouse-islands**: the most coherent board. The fog, the cream horizon and the checker grid came through in every frame, the fortress frame stays as readable as the anchor, the harbour at dawn and the battle are the two frames of the round a stranger would stop on. The sky frame is the one that could become the panorama. The palette is softer than the anchor, which is the one thing to watch against the brief's "saturated".
2. **river-town**: the closest to River Towns and Foundation, the warmest colours, the best material sheet. The fortress frame packs houses inside the walls, which is what the game does, but the cannons are hard to find among the roofs. The harbour frame went yellow. The busiest board, so the readability risk is the highest of the four.
3. **toy-harbour**: the fortress frame is the anchor repeated, which proves the reference conditioning works and adds nothing. The coast, harbour and battle frames put the island on a literal wooden table in a room, because the prompt said "on a sunlit table". That is a prompt fault, not a world. Taken as a diorama identity it is a real option, but it was not what D15 picked.
4. **shipwright-yard**: the clearest era story and the best fit for the ingredient metaphor, timber, stone and iron stacked in the yard. Also the brownest, the most cluttered and the least cosy board. The fortress frame would not read at 13 pixels.

A merge worth naming: the lighthouse islands' sea, fog and sky with the market town's roofs and material warmth. The world stays small and airy, the buildings get River Towns' saturation.

## Faults in the images

- toy-harbour: the table and the room in three frames, from "on a sunlit table" in the prompt. The fortress frame is near identical to the anchor.
- lighthouse-islands: the roof in the material sheet is a red and white checker, which belongs to the lighthouse, not to a house. The coast frame joins three islands with bridges.
- river-town: the harbour frame is yellow all over. The fortress courtyard is full, so the three cannons hide.
- shipwright-yard: the harbour frame is dark for a cosy game. The material sheet added a crane and a forge on its own, both fit.
- All four: the edit endpoint kept the anchor's low-poly look and the visible grid, so the boards differ in world and not in render style, as intended.

## Critics

Gemini ranked toy-harbour, lighthouse-islands, river-town, shipwright-yard. GLM ranked toy-harbour, river-town, shipwright-yard, lighthouse-islands. Both put toy-harbour first, both on the readability argument: the simplest ground, the strongest contrast, team colours sit on it without a fight. Both call lighthouse-islands' fog and soft palette a readability risk, Gemini as fixable, GLM as the riskiest board. Both call river-town too dense inside the walls. Both propose the same merge, toy-harbour's forms and materials with river-town's warm colours and half-timber architecture. The director ranked lighthouse-islands first, so this round the two critics agree with each other and not with the director. Second calibration point for roadmap A6, resolved by Sebastian's pick.

Note on the critics' reading: Gemini saw a wooden table edge in the toy-harbour battle frame and read it as a deliberate diorama. GLM named felt and cork as its materials. Both took a prompt fault as a design choice, which is one reason the director does not count the toy-harbour board as evidence for the toy world.

*   **Identity:** This is a world conceived as a physical board game, a collection of painted wooden blocks played with on a sunlit table.
*   **Words against images:** The frames strongly deliver on "cheerful, chunky, sunlit." The lighting is bright and direct, the shapes are simple and solid, and the palette is clean. The frame that drifts most is **battle**, which doubles down on the "toy on a table" concept by showing the real-world table edge. This is a conceptual fork in the road: is the game *styled like* a toy, or is it *literally* a toy on a table?
*   **Readability:** Excellent. The simple grass texture and clean wall/cannon models from the `fortress` frame provide a perfect, quiet canvas. At 13 pixels, these simple, high-contrast shapes will read instantly. The bright, saturated world will make deep blue, purple, teal, and olive team colours pop.
*   **Consistency:** Very high. All six frames feel like they belong to the same style guide, from the chunky clouds in the `sky` to the simple facets on the `material` rock. The lighting is consistent across all in-game views.
*   **Risk:** The biggest risk is the style becoming restrictive or childish. The "painted wood" aesthetic must be applied thoughtfully to 60 assets to avoid everything looking clumsy. More complex units or structures could lose their definition and just look like lumpy blocks.
*   **Store appeal:** Very high. The `coast` frame is instantly charming, unique, and clearly communicates "fun, accessible fortress builder." It would absolutely stand out on a store page and has a broad, welcoming appeal.
*   **Recommended edit:** Commit to the *aesthetic* of a wooden toy set, but remove the literal *context* of the tabletop. Place this toy world in a proper, stylised sea and sky (as in the `sky` frame) to maintain immersion without the distracting meta-narrative of playing a game within a game.

### 2. lighthouse-islands

*   **Identity:** This is a world of lonely, windswept outposts at the edge of a map, defined by fog, quietude, and the contrast between stark forts and an endless sea.
*   **Words against images:** "Calm, airy, remote" are perfectly captured in `coast`, `fortress` and `sky`, which evoke the client's *A Monster's Expedition* reference. The fog and desaturated palette are key. The frame that drifts most is **battle**, where the hot, fiery explosion feels tonally dissonant with the otherwise cool and placid world.
*   **Readability:** Moderate to poor. While the shapes in the `fortress` view are simple, the overall palette is muted and low-contrast. The specified team colours, particularly purple and olive, would likely look muddy and could be difficult to distinguish against the teal water and pale ground. Fog also presents a direct threat to readability.
*   **Consistency:** Mostly consistent. The mood is strong across all frames, but the `material` sheet feels a bit generic, and the `battle` frame's VFX style feels imported from a different, more action-oriented game.
*   **Risk:** The atmosphere actively working against gameplay. The fog, muted colours, and soft lighting that make this style so appealing are all enemies of clarity. In a complex battle, players could easily lose track of units and ownership status.
*   **Store appeal:** High. The `coast` and `harbour` frames are beautiful, promising a serene and thoughtful strategic experience. It would appeal strongly to fans of minimalist builders like *Bad North* or *Islanders*.
*   **Recommended edit:** Increase the base saturation of the land and water, and ensure the buildable grid area is a lighter, cleaner colour. This provides a better background for vibrant team colours to sit on, improving readability while the fog can be retained for atmosphere around the island edges.

### 3. river-town

*   **Identity:** This is a vibrant, dense, storybook world of a prosperous medieval town, where the fortress is an organic part of a larger, living community.
*   **Words against images:** The frames perfectly embody "homely, busy, storybook," directly hitting the *River Towns* reference. The density of buildings, warm tile roofs, and lively details are all present. The frame that drifts most is **sky**, which looks too much like a generic, semi-realistic skybox and lacks the painterly, illustrative quality of the town below.
*   **Readability:** Poor. This is the style's critical failure for this game genre. The `fortress` view is overwhelmed with visual noise. The mix of grass, paths, and dense, pre-existing buildings makes the underlying grid almost invisible. A 13-pixel wall would get lost immediately. Distinguishing player-built cannons from decorative chimneys would be a nightmare.
*   **Consistency:** Excellent. The architectural style, colour palette, and level of detail are incredibly coherent across all frames showing the town. The material sheet is a perfect representation of the in-game assets.
*   **Risk:** Sacrificing gameplay for beauty. The core risk is building a world that is gorgeous to look at but frustrating to play. The art style is fundamentally at odds with the core requirement of a clear, readable grid for strategic placement.
*   **Store appeal:** Very high. The `coast` and `harbour` frames are stunning and suggest a deep, high-quality city-builder. It would generate a lot of clicks, but might set false expectations about the type of gameplay.
*   **Recommended edit:** Create a "gameplay canvas" within the walls. The ground inside the buildable area must be radically simplified to a clean grid of paving stones or simple grass, removing all decorative clutter. This preserves the beautiful town aesthetic for the world while carving out a readable space for the core game loop.

### 4. shipwright-yard

*   **Identity:** This is a functional, industrial world focused on the process of construction, where the fortress is a working factory for the machinery of war.
*   **Words against images:** "Inventive, busy, warm metal" are present, especially in `coast` and `harbour` with their cranes and sunset glow. However, the frame that drifts most is **fortress**. It has lost almost all of the "shipwright" identity and just looks like a standard stone fort on a rock, failing to communicate the core theme.
*   **Readability:** Moderate. The `fortress` view has a clean grid. The main issue is the muddy, desaturated colour palette. The grey rock and dark teal water do not provide a good backdrop for the specified team colours to be clearly distinguishable, especially purple and olive.
*   **Consistency:** Weakest of the four. There's a disconnect between the dense, thematic `coast` and `harbour` views, and the generic `fortress` view. The `material` sheet shows interesting ideas like the verdigris roof that aren't prominent elsewhere. The theme feels conceptually strong but inconsistently executed.
*   **Risk:** The identity is fragile. The biggest risk is that in the process of simplifying assets for readability, all the unique "shipwright" elements (cranes, scaffolds, forges) are the first to be cut, leaving behind a generic and uninspired stone fortress.
*   **Store appeal:** Moderate. The theme is interesting for players who like logistics and industrial settings, but the visuals are less immediately charming or striking than the other candidates. It's more of a slow-burn appreciation than an instant scroll-stopper.
*   **Recommended edit:** Fully commit to the industrial theme in the gameplay space. Make the unique elements—cranes, forges, slipways—part of the buildable assets or permanent features on the grid. Brighten the palette and use more of the interesting materials, like the copper, to give the world a more distinct and readable character.

### Ranking & Recommendation

1.  **toy-harbour:** This is the strongest candidate as it best balances the client's desire for a unique, cosy style with the absolute necessity of strategic readability.
2.  **lighthouse-islands:** A beautiful and evocative choice that captures a different part of the brief well, but its atmospheric style presents a direct, though fixable, risk to gameplay clarity.
3.  **river-town:** While visually stunning, its core aesthetic of dense detail is fundamentally hostile to the readability required by a grid-based strategy game, making it a beautiful but poor fit.
4.  **shipwright-yard:** The concept is promising but the execution is inconsistent and the palette is the least appealing, risking a descent into a generic low-poly look.

A merge of **toy-harbour** and **river-town** would likely beat the best single candidate. This "Storybook Toy" merge would use the chunky, readable forms and clean material style of `toy-harbour`, but adopt the warmer, more detailed architectural language of `river-town`, such as its half-timbered walls and orange tile roofs, creating a world that is both charmingly detailed and perfectly readable.

**RANKING:** toy-harbour, lighthouse-islands, river-town, shipwright-yard

### Ranking

1. **toy-harbour** – The only candidate that natively satisfies the 13 px readability requirement and the four-team-colour brief while offering a unique, store-friendly identity that avoids both grittiness and genericism.
2. **river-town** – Visually the most “premium” and cosy, but it would need aggressive simplification to avoid becoming a pixelated mess at default zoom, and its orange-heavy palette actively fights two of the four team colours.
3. **shipwright-yard** – A strong thematic fit for “build walls and cannons,” but its industrial complexity is fundamentally unsuited to the tiny scale of a browser grid game and would bloat the asset pipeline dangerously.
4. **lighthouse-islands** – Beautiful as a mood piece, but its core aesthetic (fog, softness) is directly opposed to the core gameplay requirements (clarity, team distinction, combat visibility), making it the riskiest choice for actual development.

**Would a merge beat the best single candidate?** Yes—a **“Tabletop Storybook”** merge that takes **toy-harbour’s** chunky grid readability, physical material language (painted wood, felt, cork), and neutral team-colour base, then infuses **river-town’s** warm colour grading, half-timbered architectural variety, and cosy market-town life (simplified to flat planes in the fortress view) would create a world that plays as cleanly as a toy but feels as rich and inhabited as a storybook village.
