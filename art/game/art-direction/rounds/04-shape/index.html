# Round 04 notes

Phase C, shape language and detail level. The fortress corner of the colour anchor, [refs/corner.png](refs/corner.png), redrawn six times through `google/nano-banana-2.1/edit` with one detail reference attached per level, see [round.json](round.json): the Kingdoms and Castles screenshot, the River Towns screenshot, the Foundation screenshot, each with its interface cropped off, in [refs/](refs). Two languages per level, rounded and chunky against faceted and sharp. Then one sheet of the shipwright's structures in the toy language from [structures.png](../../anchors/structures.png), one river map and one land map from the colour frame. 9 images, 1.35 USD, plus four critiques, two of them on a wrong test, see below.

The 13 pixel test is [corner-13px.png](corner-13px.png): every edit scaled so a tile is 24.7 px wide, the default view of the gameplay contract, magnified four times without smoothing. [maps-13px.png](maps-13px.png) does the same for the three maps.

## The test was at half scale, now fixed

The round 03 test and the first sheet of this round scaled the frame so the fortress wall was 13 px tall, taking the wall for one metre. A ruler on the anchor says a tile is about 52 px wide there and the fortress wall about two tiles tall, so the frames were shown at under half the game's size. [teamcrop.py](../../../../pipeline/teamcrop.py) and the new [px13.py](../../../../pipeline/px13.py) now scale by the tile width, the anchor's [team_13px.png](../../anchors/team_13px.png) is re-rendered, and the two critiques on the half-scale sheet sit in [critique/half-scale/](critique/half-scale), ranking 2, 4, 6, 1, 3, 5 both. The team-colour verdict of round 03 stands, the base blocks were drawn at the real tile size and only the frame around them was small.

## Director's read, provisional

The edit kept the camera, the palette and every object in place in all six. The difference between the languages is bigger than the difference between the levels.

- **Rounded and chunky, 1, 3, 5** overshoot. The prompt asked for a bevel of a tenth of a block and the model made a quarter: the merlons are pillows, the grid softens, the keep turns to clay. Painted wood became plasticine. At the default view the walls still read, the notched top edge is softer than the anchor's.
- **Faceted and sharp, 2, 4, 6** are the anchor's own language. 2 is nearly the anchor itself: the anchor already has hard edges with a small chamfer. The chamfer is what makes the toy, so a hard zero bevel would lose it; the right number sits between the two columns, about 0.05 m.
- **Kingdoms and Castles, 1 and 2**: houses lose their door, the walls stay the same. Nothing is missing at the default view, the zoomed view is bare.
- **River Towns, 3 and 4**: a door, two windows, a chimney, crates by the gate. The walls stay plain slabs. At the default view the features are dots that read as life, not as noise.
- **Foundation, 5 and 6**: timber framing, tile ridges, a well, a cart, ball stacks, a balcony on the tower. The keep became a half-timbered house, a drift. At the default view the props are two-pixel lumps in the yard; zoomed in it is the ceiling of what the budget allows.

Director's ranking for the game: 4, 2, 6, 3, 1, 5. Level River Towns, language faceted and sharp with the anchor's chamfer.

## The structures

All eight read as what they are at full size: crane, slipway ribs, scaffold, tower, forge, timber, stone, iron. Two faults. The tower carries drawn stone courses, more than the fortress walls next to it, so the tower must follow the wall rule. The scaffold is thin poles, finer than any feature the default view can show, so it lives in the zoomed view or gets thick posts. The slipway ribs become one orange shape at the default view, which is fine for a yard landmark.

## The maps

Both hold. The river map reads as the same world with the river as a tile kind, banks of sand, one bridge. The land map reads too: grass, sand patches and grey rock tiles carry the grid without the sea. The weakest edge is the cream wall against the yellow sand path, on both maps and on the anchor. The lighting phase owes every wall a base shadow on its ground tile, which is the rule that separates stone from sand at 13 px.

## Faults in the images

- The six lost the small trees and tufts by the gate, and the chunky three lost the tile lines under the softened grass.
- Foundation replaced the keep's stone front with timber framing in both 5 and 6, a drift the prompt did not ask for.
- The structures sheet drew the wood lighter than the palette's wood swatch. The sheet is a shape reference, not a colour one.

Critiques

gemini (google/gemini-2.5-pro)
Here is the critique.

1.  **Edits vs. Anchor:**
    *   **1 & 2:** Added small red flags on the corner towers and changed the gatehouse roof from a hip roof to a gable roof. At 13 pixels, the rounded corners of **1** blur the battlements into a single soft line, unlike the anchor's more defined, if still soft, crenellations.
    *   **3 & 4:** Added a door and chimney to the main building and crates in the yard. These details are completely lost at 13 pixels, becoming indistinct noise.
    *   **5 & 6:** Added a well, a cart, shutters, and timber framing. The cart in both **5** and **6** adds high-contrast clutter outside the wall, confusing its silhouette at 13 pixels. In **5**, the rounded walls and the cart combine to make the perimeter almost unreadable.

2.  **Readability at 13 Pixels:**
    *   The **faceted/sharp** language (2, 4, 6) keeps the wall edge, battlements, and gate readable. The **rounded/chunky** language (1, 3, 5) loses them to aliasing and softness, making the silhouette weak and blurry.
    *   The middle detail level (3, 4) adds nothing visible at 13 pixels; the door, chimney and crates are indistinguishable from the low-detail versions.
    *   For this game, a wall at 13 pixels must show a clean, high-contrast silhouette against the ground, the rhythmic pattern of its battlements, and the clear void of its gate.

3.  **Yard Structures:**
    *   The **scaffold** would not read as a distinct structure at default view; its thin, crisscrossing lines would dissolve into a noisy texture or be mistaken for ruins. The **forge** would read as a house with a chimney, not a specific workshop.
    *   The **crane** (with its complex joinery), the **watchtower** (with its individual stone blocks), and the **forge** (with its brick-built chimney) all carry finer geometric detail than the fortress walls they would stand beside, even the high-detail versions.
    *   I would drop the **scaffold** for its poor readability and ambiguous silhouette.

4.  **Maps:**
    *   The look holds on the river map.
    *   On the pure land map, the readability breaks immediately. The first thing to fail is the contrast between the **cream stone walls and the yellow sand paths**. The two light-coloured materials bleed into one another, destroying the fortress's clear outline, which the dark blue sea previously guaranteed.

5.  **Geometry Rules:**
    1.  Bevel width for all hard edges must be under 1/16th of a tile's width to maintain a crisp silhouette at distance.
    2.  The middle detail level should be built from no more than 5-7 discrete parts per building (e.g., 1 base, 1 roof, 1 door, 1 chimney, 3 window shutters).
    3.  At 13 pixels, the wall shows only its mass, silhouette, and battlements. At 96 pixels, details like individual stone courses, material textures, and arrow slits can appear.

6.  **Readability Ranking (Best to Worst):**
    *   **2:** The faceted shape and low detail provide the absolute clearest, most readable silhouette of the fortress's form and function.
    *   **4:** Nearly as readable as 2; the extra medium details are invisible noise at this scale but do not harm the strong outer perimeter.
    *   **1:** The chunky form harms readability, but the low detail level at least prevents additional visual clutter from making it worse.
    *   **6:** While the faceted walls are sharp, the high-detail props outside the walls actively fight the silhouette, creating confusion.
    *   **3:** The weak, rounded silhouette is made slightly worse by the addition of interior detail that reads only as muddy noise.
    *   **5:** This is the least readable, combining the blurry silhouette of the chunky style with high-contrast external clutter that breaks the perimeter line.

    RANKING: 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 5
glm (glm-5v-turbo)
1. **1** Drift: Camera tilted higher and pulled back, warming the colour temperature; the wall thickness increased beyond anchor proportions. Reads: The fat bevels blur the battlement crenellations into a single wavy line at 13px, losing the gap rhythm that defines the parapet. **2** Drift: Camera pushed lower and closer, shifting perspective to show more roof topside; contrast increased. Reads: The sharp gate arch collapses to a 2-pixel black notch that merges with the wall’s base shadow, making the entrance invisible without the anchor’s soft gradient to carve depth. **3** Drift: Camera rotated 15° clockwise; added a brown door, two dark window squares, a chimney pipe, and three crate clusters beyond the specified “middle” scope. Reads: The window dots vibrate against the cream wall at 13px, creating false texture that competes with the cannon line inside the courtyard. **4** Drift: Same object additions as 3; lighting flattened to remove ambient occlusion. Reads: The door recess becomes a hard black rectangle that reads as a hole in the wall rather than an opening, breaking the fortress’s solid-barrier silhouette. **5** Drift: Camera moved to a three-quarter view showing the dock side; added timber framing on the gable, terracotta tile ridges, shutters, a stone well, a wooden cart, an anvil, and scattered barrels—exceeding the “most detail” brief into clutter. Reads: The vertical timber posts fragment the wall mass into stripes; at 13px the wall ceases to read as a continuous defensive structure and becomes a fence. **6** Drift: Same prop overload as 5; colour desaturated toward grey-brown. Reads: Slightly sharper than 5, but the shutter gaps create false negative spaces that suggest the wall is broken or incomplete at default zoom.

2. **Rounded/chunky (odd numbers)** keeps the wall edge, battlements, and gate readable because the bevel creates a persistent light-top/shaded-side model that separates the mass from grass and sand even when undersampled. **Faceted/sharp (even numbers)** loses them: the 1‑pixel wall edge falls between screen pixels or aliases into the background, and the gate arch becomes geometrically ambiguous without the soft gradient to define the void. **Detail levels 5 and 6 (Foundation)** add nothing visible at 13 pixels—every timber, shutter, well, cart, and tile ridge vanishes into sub‑pixel mud, leaving only visual noise that actively harms the silhouette. **At 13 pixels a wall must show exactly three distinct tonal planes—a lit top, a shaded front face, and a dark base or outline—and nothing else.**

3. The **slipway** (orange boat hull) would not read at default view; it resolves as an amorphous orange blob or a pile of curved wood, identifiable only on hover or at 96px. The **crane** (with its thin jib, rope, and counterweight), **slipway** (segmented curvature), and **forge** (chimney, fire glow, and door recession) all carry significantly more geometric and texel detail than the adjacent fortress wall slabs, creating an inconsistent density budget. **Drop the slipway:** it demands the highest polycount for the least recognizable silhouette at gameplay distance, its nautical specificity fights the universal tileset requirement, and its colour (orange) clashes with the roof palette without adding clear functional legibility.

4. The look **holds on the river map**—the blue channel provides the same cool complementary contrast as the sea, and the bridge introduces a new linear element that suits the chunky geometry. **On the pure land map the look holds but strains; the first thing that breaks is the loss of the high‑contrast blue coastline**, which previously framed the composition and separated the cream fortress from the background; without it, the fortress walls visually bleed into the yellow sand paths and the light green grass at the screen periphery, reducing the “pop” that makes the island map instantly readable and forcing the eye to work harder to find the focal point.

5. **Rule 1 – Bevel:** Set bevel width to **0.12–0.15** (12–15%) of a one‑metre tile width for chunky styles; for sharp styles set bevel to **0** but enforce a minimum **2‑pixel** face width at 96ppm to prevent collapse. **Rule 2 – Part Count:** Cap middle‑level (River Towns) buildings at **7–9** discrete mesh parts: wall base, roof volume, door plane, 2 window planes, chimney cylinder, and 1–2 prop attachment points (crate stack or barrel cluster). **Rule 3 – LOD Split:** At **13 pixels** (default view) the wall must render only **primary mass** (top face, front face, base shadow); at **96 pixels** (zoomed) it may reveal **secondary detail** (door inset, window holes, texture trim, shutters, and placed props).

6. **1** excels because the fat bevels guarantee the wall reads as a solid, unbroken barrier even at 12px height, and the simplicity keeps the courtyard contents (cannons, roofs) clearly subordinate. **2** trades slight robustness for crisp silhouette definition, giving excellent edge detection against grass, though the gate suffers without soft shading. **3** retains the readable chunky mass but the added door and windows introduce dark specks that distract from the essential fortress shape. **4** combines medium detail with sharp edges, causing window frames to break the horizontal wall line and create visual static that confuses the parapet rhythm. **6** survives the detail overload better than its chunky twin because hard edges separate the timber and shutters slightly, yet the wall still fragments into a busy pattern rather than a single defensive form. **5** fails completely—the soft, thick geometry blurs together with the high‑frequency detail of tile ridges and timber posts, turning the fortress into an illegible beige lump where the wall loses its edge and the interior objects vanish.  

RANKING: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 5
